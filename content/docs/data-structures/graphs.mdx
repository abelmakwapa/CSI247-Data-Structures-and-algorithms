## Definition and intuition

<AcademicPanel tone="definition" title="Mental model">
A graph is a set of vertices connected by edges. Edges can be directed or undirected, weighted or unweighted. Graphs do not need one root, and cycles are normal.
</AcademicPanel>

<AcademicPanel tone="theorem" title="Remember this">
If the problem says “connected,” “reachable,” or “shortest path,” think graph.
</AcademicPanel>

## The problem it solves

Reach for a graph when entities connect in more than one direction or through cycles.

### Where it appears

- maps
- social feeds
- dependencies
- recommendations

## Real-world analogy

<AcademicPanel tone="pro" title="Maps and social networks">
Cities are nodes and roads are edges; friends are nodes and relationships are edges. The connections are the data.
</AcademicPanel>

## Trade-offs

### Strengths

- Models arbitrary relationships
- Many powerful algorithms compose on it

### Watch-outs

- Representation choice changes performance
- Cycles require visited-state discipline

## Common mistakes

<AcademicPanel tone="warning" title="Pressure-test the trade-off">
If the problem says “connected,” “reachable,” or “shortest path,” think graph. When the workload changes, revisit whether the structure's invariant still matches the problem.
</AcademicPanel>

## Interview lens

### What a strong answer notices

- Choose adjacency list for sparse graphs; matrix for dense or instant edge checks.
- Mark visited when you enqueue/push, not only when you remove.
- Separate connectivity, shortest path, and ordering as different questions.

### Questions worth rehearsing

- When is an adjacency matrix the right choice?
- Why must graph traversal track visited nodes?
